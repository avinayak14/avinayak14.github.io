&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Within the last year, and especially the last few months, big tech has been plagued by scandals our 24-hour news cycle is keen to cover. From Facebook's privacy violations and concealment of Russian meddling in the U.S. elections, to Google's recent <a href = "https://thehill.com/policy/technology/420838-google-ceo-responds-to-steve-king-concerns-about-granddaughters-iphone" target="_blank"> joke of a hearing</a>, tech companies and their executives are under a growing spotlight. Market value reflects the sentiment: Facebook's 50-day SMA stock value has dipped nearly 30% in five months, while Amazon's and Nvidia's have dropped 15% and 35% respectively <a href = "https://www.nasdaq.com/symbol/amzn/stock-chart?&intraday=off&timeframe=1y&splits=off&movingaverage=50day&lowerstudy=volume&comparison=off" target="_blank">since October</a>. One company, though, has held its fort steady, its 50-day SMA down only a few points from an October all-time high. Here's why one characteristic of that company will let it dominate without fear over the next decade.
<br/>
<br/>
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under Steve Ballmer, Microsoft had failures, the biggest arguably being its lack of initiative on <a href = "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1mrKy1ixKaQ" target="_blank">mobile</a>. But in the last four years under Satya Nadella, the company has turned sharply positive. Commercial cloud revenue grew from a $4.4 billion annual run rate in 2014 to over $23 billion in 2018. Cash dividends per share have nearly doubled, from $0.92 to $1.68. And the company has poured talent into its research arm, MSR, funding big bets in AI and quantum computing. What will keep propelling it forward, though, is its diverse palette of revenue streams.
<br/>
<br/>
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Facebook and Google make 97% and 88% of their revenue from advertising respectively, and already dominate between 60% and 70% of U.S. ad market share — leaving little room to grow in that vertical. Given their track record, they'll likely find ways around this, but the problems ahead are hard, both technically and on the business side (ever-more-creative monetization strategies, for one). Amazon and Apple both draw over 60% of annual revenue from retail product sales and iPhones, respectively, and with the mixed reception of the iPhone X series, it's easy to see why Apple worries about sustaining YoY sales at ever-rising price points. Microsoft, however, has no revenue category above 30% of annual revenue. Sources include hardware (the Surface, Windows machines, the Xbox), the Office 365 subscription model, ads, Windows Server, and Azure. Unlike the other mega-corps, the decay of any single stream wouldn't seriously threaten its financial sustainability.
<br/>
<br/>
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Revenue can seem a shallow metric when evaluating a tech company. "Isn't product, user experience, and growth more important?", one might ask. Plenty of startups and unicorns rack up large debt, relying purely on equity funding in their beginnings. But while revenue matters less for startups, it matters enormously for tech giants — especially as user engagement plateaus, in-house hardware becomes more desirable, and competitive labor markets demand paying for scarce talent without restraint. Access to additional GPUs for training machine learning models requires capital. Offering highly competitive salaries to lure PhDs away from academia, or fend off a heavy offer from Uber ATG, requires capital. Building hardware in-house for projects like Oculus, Hololens, and self-driving cars at global scale requires capital. And in the fight for increasingly hardware-based innovation, diversified revenue can only help.
